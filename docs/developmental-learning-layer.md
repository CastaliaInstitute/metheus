# Developmental psychology and the learning layer

**Status:** working design, version 0.1. This layer uses developmental and learning sciences to help a learner, family, or educator choose and revise learning supports. Promethean features may inform reflection when the learner wants that; they do not define ability, developmental level, diagnosis, or a required teaching method.

## Purpose and boundary

Metheus should bring three distinct views into a learning reflection:

1. **Promethean design:** a symbolic language the person may find meaningful. It can suggest a question about motivation, agency, attention, decision-making, or preferred conditions.
2. **Developmental and learning context:** the learner's age/stage when relevant, prior knowledge, current goal, culture/language, relationships, access needs, health and energy context, and the demands of the skill being learned.
3. **Learning experience over time:** what task and supports were actually used, what the learner reported, and what they later demonstrated or transferred—with the source and method visible.

The output is a revisable set of options and questions, not a learning prescription. A chart feature must never determine school placement, curriculum access, ability, disability status, career suitability, or expectations for a child. Developmental norms are broad context, not a timetable against which to rank an individual.

To honor someone's Promethean design, preserve the name and lineage of the symbolic language they chose, ask what—if anything—they find useful in it, and let that reflection inform their own question. The learner can revise or reject the interpretation at any time. No content, support, or opportunity is gated by a chart feature.

## Evidence-informed design principles

### Development is dynamic and situated

Learning and development change across the lifespan and reflect interactions among biology, prior experience, motivation, social relationships, culture, the learning environment, and time. Effective instruction depends on the learner, the content, and the setting together; a single profile cannot capture that system. [National Academies, *How People Learn II*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/2), [development and learning across the lifespan](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/24783/chapter/3).

### Start with the learning goal and task

Different tasks call for different forms of representation and practice. A learner may need a worked demonstration to begin a motor task, discussion to clarify an idea, accessible text to study a concept, repeated retrieval to strengthen recall, or a combination. These are task and access choices, not evidence of a fixed “visual,” “auditory,” or “kinesthetic” type.

### Offer meaningful options and access

The CAST Universal Design for Learning (UDL) framework organizes design around multiple means of **engagement**, **representation**, and **action and expression**. Metheus can use this as an option-generating framework: what could make the task meaningful and reachable, how might the information be represented accessibly, and how else could understanding be shown? UDL is a planning framework, not proof that any particular option will work for an individual; the learner's feedback and task-relevant evidence guide revision. [CAST UDL Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/).

### Do not turn design into a fixed learning style

The evidence about matching instruction to a declared modality preference is mixed. A 2024 meta-analysis found a modest pooled estimate across 21 eligible studies (1,712 participants), but substantial variability and infrequent crossover effects led the authors to conclude that a benefit is far from conclusive. Metheus should therefore record preferences as revisable learner feedback, not classify a person or promise that matching a symbolic type or sensory preference improves learning. [Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11270031/).

## Developmental lens

Use stage as context for what supports may be available and appropriate, not as a fixed capacity label. Observe a learner's actual communication, attention, regulation, motor access, prior experience, and interest. When age-linked concerns arise, Metheus can help organize observations and questions for an educator, pediatrician, or developmental professional; it cannot screen or diagnose.

| Life context | Learning lens | Metheus can help notice | Metheus must not do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prenatal / newborn | Care, sensory environment, health, and caregiver context; no curriculum or performance frame | Caregiver questions and relevant context when voluntarily shared | Attribute learning capacity, chart traits, or future ability to an infant |
| Infancy | Responsive interaction, attention to the infant's cues, safe exploration, play, sensory and motor experience, communication, and co-regulation | Caregiver-reported moments of shared attention, turn-taking, exploration, and changing interests | Turn milestones into a score, drill an infant, infer inner states from a chart, or compare babies competitively |
| Early / middle childhood | Play, language, relationships, growing knowledge and skills, supportive structure, choice, and accessible practice | What the child is exploring, what assistance is useful, what the child says or shows, and contextual constraints | Set expectations from Human Design features or treat a broad age range as a deadline |
| Adolescence | Increasing autonomy and identity development alongside ongoing variation in executive skills, social context, sleep, and stress | Learner-set goals, chosen supports, changing responsibilities, and privacy/assent | Override the young person's voice with a parent or chart interpretation |
| Adulthood / later life | Lifelong learning shaped by prior knowledge, goals, context, health, access, and experience | The learner's aims, accommodations, preferred options, practice, and task-specific progress | Assume plasticity ends at a particular age or reduce changes to hormones or personality |

For babies, foreground responsive back-and-forth interaction and safe exploration. In “serve and return,” an infant's gesture, gaze, vocalization, or action is met by an adult's attentive response, with the adult following the child's cue and allowing turns. Metheus may help caregivers notice these everyday exchanges and their own context, but it should not turn them into a performance target or infer a teaching prescription from a chart. [Harvard Center on the Developing Child: Serve and Return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resource-guides/guide-serve-and-return/). Developmental monitoring and formal screening are distinct; concerns should be discussed with the child's healthcare provider. [CDC: Developmental monitoring and screening](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/about/developmental-monitoring-and-screening.html).

## Learning reflection workflow

1. **Ask the learner's question.** Identify what they want to learn, do, understand, or make. For a child, include their voice in an age- and communication-appropriate way; a caregiver's goal is not automatically the child's goal.
2. **Name the task and context.** Separate the skill/content from setting, prior knowledge, language, available time, fatigue, materials, accessibility, social supports, and other relevant circumstances.
3. **Offer options, not labels.** Suggest a small set of approaches that fit the goal and access needs. A Promethean feature may be included as an optional reflective prompt (for example, “Does this design theme make experimentation feel inviting, or is another approach more useful today?”). It cannot eliminate options or assign a learning style.
4. **Record the activity.** Capture the task, approaches used, support level, learner choice, and access supports. Do not require sensitive diagnoses or a detailed personal profile.
5. **Record feedback and evidence separately.** Learner feedback about interest, effort, or clarity and observations of recall, demonstration, problem solving, transfer, or difficulty are separate records with their own source, time, and method. A learner saying they enjoyed a lesson is valuable feedback, but it is not by itself evidence of mastery; a score alone is not the whole learning experience.
6. **Review and revise collaboratively.** Compare like tasks where possible, look for change over time, preserve missingness and alternative explanations, and ask what the learner wants to keep or change. Do not claim that a chart feature or one teaching method caused success.

## Example: design as reflection, not placement

**Learner's question:** “How could I get more comfortable speaking French?”

**Promethean context:** the learner says a symbolic theme about responding to experience feels meaningful. The reading can ask whether the learner wants low-pressure opportunities to respond in the moment. It cannot conclude that spontaneous conversation is the learner's natural or only way to learn.

**Learning context:** the task is conversational retrieval and listening comprehension; the learner wants to improve, prefers a supportive partner, and reports that a short preparation window helps. Options could include brief retrieval practice, listening to a short dialogue, a prepared exchange, and then an optional unscripted exchange. The learner can choose, change, or reject these options.

**Review:** separately record which activities occurred, how much prompting was used, the learner's report of effort and confidence, and a task-relevant demonstration over time. Keep the interpretation provisional and adjust supports based on what the learner says and does.

## Schema and implementation

The observation schema's `developmentContext` can carry an optional life-stage label and, when useful, a reported or method-described age in days anchored to the observation's time start, with its precision marked. Do not translate an approximate age in months into an exact day count. Prenatal gestational age is kept distinct from chronological age. Corrected age is optional, limited to newborn/infancy context, paired with chronological age, and must name how its source obtained it; Metheus does not calculate it or use it to set expectations. The American Academy of Pediatrics describes corrected age as a way to account for prematurity when considering developmental timing; this context still does not make a milestone a deadline or a screening result ([AAP: Corrected Age for Preemies](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/baby/preemie/pages/Corrected-Age-For-Preemies.aspx)). An age value is not required to record a learning activity or observation.

The observation schema's `learningActivity` object records the task or skill, activity kind, approaches, support level, learner choice, access supports, and setting. `learning_activity` requires this object and `domain: "learning"`. Learner feedback and evidence about learning are recorded separately as observations with their own source, time, and method. The schema deliberately has no `learningStyle`, chart-derived ability, developmental score, or prescribed approach field.

The [synthetic learning activity](../examples/learning-activity.example.json) records what the learner practiced and how it was supported; it does not show mastery. A separately sourced [learner feedback observation](../examples/learner-feedback.example.json) and [Promethean feature](../examples/promethean-feature.example.json) feed a [structured sample reading](../examples/learning-reading.example.json). Together, the examples show cross-layer provenance without using the chart to choose a teaching method.

A future learning reader should return: (a) learner-stated goal, (b) developmental/context notes the learner chose to include, (c) symbolic prompt and source if requested, (d) options considered and chosen, (e) observed evidence and coverage, and (f) questions for revision. It should not turn the output into a diagnosis, placement, ranking, or automated curriculum decision.
